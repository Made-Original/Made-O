A PDF price list costs almost nothing to produce. Someone updates a spreadsheet, exports it, and emails it to the dealer network. Compared with building a digital catalogue, it looks like the obvious choice.

The cost of a PDF does not show up when you make it. It shows up later, spread across sales, engineering, service and finance, where nobody adds it up.

## Where PDF price lists cost money

**Outdated versions in circulation.** Once a PDF is emailed, you cannot update it. Dealers keep using the version they have, sometimes for years. Every price change creates a period where some quotes are wrong.

**Wrong orders.** When the price list, the data sheet and the catalogue are separate documents, they drift apart. A dealer orders a variant that was discontinued, or with an option that no longer fits. Each wrong order costs a correction, a delay and some trust.

**Support calls and emails.** A PDF answers the questions it was designed for. Every other question (is this pump available in stainless steel, what is the delivery time, where is the curve for this impeller) becomes an email to your sales or engineering team.

**Reprints and redistribution.** Every update means producing, checking and sending the whole document again, often in several languages.

**No visibility.** You have no idea which products dealers look at, which pages they use, or whether they opened the new price list at all.

## What a digital catalogue changes

A digital catalogue reads from one product database. When a price, a specification or a document changes, it changes for everyone at once.

- Dealers always see the current prices and products, with their own discounts.
- Specifications, curves and documents are attached to each product, so fewer questions reach your team.
- Search and filters replace page-flipping.
- New markets need translations and pricing, not a new document.
- You can see what dealers search for and which products they look at.

## A simple way to estimate your own cost

Every business is different, so the most useful comparison is your own. Ask your team to estimate, for one month:

1. How many dealer emails or calls asked for information that should have been in the catalogue?
2. How long does each one take to answer, including finding the right document?
3. How many orders or quotes needed correcting because of outdated information?
4. How many hours were spent updating, checking and sending price lists and catalogues?

For example, if a team answers 200 information requests a month at 15 minutes each, that is 50 hours a month of skilled time, before counting a single wrong order. Even a partial reduction usually covers the cost of a digital catalogue within the first year.

## When a PDF is still fine

If your range is small, prices rarely change and you sell through a handful of dealers you speak to every week, a well-maintained PDF may be enough. The case for a digital catalogue grows with the size of your range, the number of variants, the number of dealers and the number of markets.

## The middle path

You do not have to choose between a PDF and a full platform on day one. Many manufacturers start by structuring the product data for one product family and publishing it as a simple online catalogue, while keeping PDFs for the rest. Once the data is structured, generating PDFs from it is easy too, so they are never out of date.
