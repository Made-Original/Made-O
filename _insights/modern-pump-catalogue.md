Most pump catalogues are still built the way they were twenty years ago: a thick PDF of curves and tables, a separate price list, and a set of data sheets that live somewhere on a shared drive. They worked when every enquiry came through a sales engineer. They work less well when dealers in three countries need answers today.

A modern pump catalogue is not a nicer PDF. It is a different way of organising pump information so that dealers, customers and your own engineers can find the right pump without waiting for someone to look it up.

## 1. Structured pump data, not pages

The foundation is a single database of your range. Every model, size, impeller diameter, speed, motor rating, material of construction, seal option and connection is stored as data, with consistent units and names.

This sounds like housekeeping, but it changes everything downstream. When a material grade or motor rating changes, it changes once, and every catalogue, data sheet and price list that uses it is updated. When you enter a new market, you translate the attribute names once, not every page of every brochure.

## 2. Selection by duty point

Buyers rarely start with a model number. They start with a requirement: a flow rate, a head, a liquid, a temperature. A modern catalogue lets them enter that duty point and returns the pumps that meet it, with the operating point marked on each curve.

To do that, performance curves need to be stored as data (points or equations for head, efficiency, power and NPSH), not as images. Once they are, selection becomes a filter, and dealers can answer most enquiries themselves.

## 3. Curves and documents attached to the product

Every pump in the catalogue should carry its own performance curves, dimensional drawings, data sheet, manual and certificates. Not a link to a folder, but the right version of each document attached to the exact model and variant.

This is where many catalogues fall down. A dealer finds the right pump, then emails the factory for the curve and the manual. If the documents are attached to the product, that email never needs to be sent.

## 4. Pricing and access per dealer

Different dealers often see different prices, discounts and product ranges. A modern catalogue handles this with logins: each dealer sees their own pricing and the products they are allowed to sell, and the price list is always the current one. No more quotes built on a spreadsheet from two years ago.

## 5. Ready for export markets

Export customers expect the catalogue in their language, in their units, with the certifications their market requires. If your data is structured, adding a market means adding translations, unit conversions and the right document set, not producing a new catalogue from scratch.

## 6. Built to change

Pump ranges change constantly: new sizes, new motors, new materials, discontinued models. A modern catalogue is maintained by updating data, so the catalogue is never "finished" and never out of date.

## Where to start

You do not need to rebuild everything at once. Start with one product series that sells well and generates the most enquiries:

1. Collect the curves, data sheets and price lists for that series.
2. Define the attributes that matter for selection and quoting.
3. Structure the data and attach the documents.
4. Put it in front of a few dealers and see which questions they still ask.

That first series shows what the rest of the range needs, and gives your dealers something useful in weeks rather than months.
